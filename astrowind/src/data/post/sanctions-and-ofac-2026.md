## Real-World Impact on Importers and Freight Forwarders

For importers and freight forwarders, the escalating sanctions environment translates directly into tangible operational risks and significant financial exposure. The days of simply checking a list once are long gone; today's environment demands continuous, robust vigilance.

One of the most immediate business impacts is the potential for **shipment delays and seizures**. If a shipment involves a sanctioned entity, a vessel linked to a sanctioned owner, or goods destined for a prohibited end-user or jurisdiction, it faces immediate detention by customs authorities. This doesn't just halt the flow of goods; it can lead to spoilage of perishables, missed deadlines, breach of contract, and ultimately, loss of customer trust. Freight forwarders, as facilitators of these movements, bear a heavy responsibility for ensuring the legality of every transaction.

Beyond delays, the **financial exposure** is immense. Monetary penalties are the most obvious threat. As highlighted by JD Supra, 2025 witnessed OFAC taking "Action Against a Freight Forwarder" as part of its enforcement spotlight (Source 5). While the specific details vary, such actions often stem from insufficient due diligence regarding counterparties, transshipment points, or ultimate consignees. These penalties can range from hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of dollars, depending on the severity and nature of the violation. Beyond direct fines, companies incur substantial legal fees to defend against allegations, reputational damage that can impact future business, and the costly process of remediation and implementation of new compliance programs.

**Operational risks** also multiply. Vetting every partner in the supply chain – from upstream suppliers and manufacturers to carriers, consignees, and banks – becomes a complex, resource-intensive task. The rise of "ghost fleets" and intricate corporate ownership structures designed to obscure beneficial ownership makes identification incredibly challenging. Furthermore, the use of virtual assets in trade finance, while offering efficiency, now carries an amplified risk due to increased regulatory scrutiny and enforcement actions against virtual asset trading platforms, as documented by Corporate Compliance Insights (Source 6). Importers must now scrutinize not just the physical goods and parties, but also the payment mechanisms. A misstep can lead to frozen funds, blacklisted operations, and severe disruptions to global supply chains.

## Why Customs Brokers and Compliance Teams Are on Alert

Customs brokers and the dedicated compliance teams within freight forwarding and import companies are unequivocally on high alert in March 2026. The regulatory pendulum has swung decisively towards increased accountability for intermediaries, fundamentally shifting the landscape of liability.

The most significant change is the **shift in liability**. Regulators like OFAC are increasingly holding customs brokers and freight forwarders responsible for sanctions violations, even when they may not be the direct party to the prohibited transaction. The expectation is that these trade facilitators, by virtue of their expertise and position in the supply chain, possess the capability and responsibility to conduct thorough due diligence. This means a broker cannot simply rely on information provided by the importer if red flags are present or if their own screening processes are inadequate. The "Action Against a Freight Forwarder" noted by JD Supra in 2025 serves as a stark reminder that these intermediaries are no longer just pass-through entities but critical gatekeepers in the sanctions enforcement ecosystem (Source 5).

This increased liability necessitates far more stringent **documentation requirements**. Every step of the transaction – from initial inquiry to final delivery – must be meticulously documented to demonstrate due diligence efforts. This includes records of sanctions screening results, communications with parties, internal risk assessments, and any remedial actions taken. In the event of an audit or investigation, the ability to present a clear, comprehensive, and defensible audit trail is paramount. Without it, companies risk being unable to prove they acted responsibly, potentially leading to findings of "willful blindness" or "reckless disregard." Foley Hoag’s "2025 in Review" noted the increasing importance of robust record-keeping as a key defense strategy (Source 3).

Finally, the pervasive threat of **audit risk** is a constant motivator. OFAC, along with other agencies, is actively conducting audits and investigations, often triggered by tips, suspicious activity reports (SARs), or routine data analysis. The penalties for violations discovered during an audit can be severe, compounding the financial and reputational damage. Compliance teams are under immense pressure to implement proactive, robust internal controls, conduct regular internal audits, and ensure continuous training for all relevant personnel. The goal is not just to detect violations but to prevent them altogether, demonstrating a culture of compliance that can mitigate penalties should an inadvertent issue arise.

## How to Stay Compliant: 5 Steps for 2026

1.  **Implement Robust, Real-Time Screening Protocols:** Regularly screen all counterparties – including customers, suppliers, banks, vessel owners, and even individuals within organizations – against consolidated sanctions lists (OFAC SDN, EU, UN, etc.). This isn't a one-time check; ongoing monitoring is crucial, as designations can change weekly, as evidenced by Steptoe's "Weekly Sanctions Update" (Source 1).
2.  **Conduct Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) on High-Risk Transactions:** For any transaction involving high-risk jurisdictions, complex ownership structures, or dual-use goods, conduct deeper investigations. This includes understanding the ultimate beneficial owner (UBO), verifying end-user certificates, and scrutinizing payment methods, especially if virtual assets are involved, given the recent FinCEN/DOJ focus (Source 6).
3.  **Develop and Maintain a Comprehensive Sanctions Compliance Program (SCP):** Your SCP should be tailored to your business, regularly updated, and clearly communicated. It must include clear policies, procedures, risk assessments, internal controls, management commitment, and ongoing employee training to foster a strong culture of compliance.
4.  **Leverage Technology for Automation and Accuracy:** Manual screening is prone to error and cannot keep pace with the dynamic nature of sanctions. Utilize AI-powered compliance solutions to automate screening, reduce false positives, identify hidden connections, and provide an auditable record of all due diligence activities, drastically improving efficiency and reducing human error.
5.  **Stay Informed on Regulatory Changes and Enforcement Trends:** Consistently monitor advisories, enforcement actions, and guidance issued by OFAC, EU, UN, and other relevant authorities. Subscribing to trade compliance updates, attending webinars, and consulting with legal experts are vital to adapting your compliance program to the evolving landscape, as outlined in "The State of OFAC Sanctions Enforcement in 2025-26" (Source 2).

### What are the biggest Sanctions & OFAC risks in 2026?
The biggest risks in 2026 include the rapid pace of new designations targeting Russia and its facilitators, increasing scrutiny on virtual asset transactions used for sanctions evasion, and a broader interpretation of "facilitation" that holds intermediaries like freight forwarders more accountable. ### What fines and penalties apply?
Fines and penalties for sanctions violations are severe and can vary widely based on the jurisdiction, the nature of the violation (civil vs. criminal), and whether it was willful. OFAC civil penalties can range from thousands to hundreds of millions of dollars per violation, with "willful" violations incurring even higher criminal penalties, including imprisonment for individuals. For instance, 2025 saw OFAC issue a penalty in the tens of millions for a single company, and various enforcement actions against virtual asset platforms often involve multi-million dollar settlements (Source 5, 6). The statutory maximum penalties for OFAC violations can reach up to $356,574 per violation or twice the amount of the underlying transaction, whichever is greater, alongside potential criminal charges that carry separate, substantial monetary penalties and lengthy prison sentences.
