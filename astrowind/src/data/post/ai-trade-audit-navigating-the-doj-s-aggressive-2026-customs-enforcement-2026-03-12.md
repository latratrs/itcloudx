## Overview

The trade compliance landscape for U.S. importers in 2026 is defined by an unprecedented level of scrutiny and enforcement risk. A significant shift in federal strategy, led by the **Department of Justice (DOJ)**, has moved beyond traditional customs penalties and into the high-stakes realm of civil fraud litigation. The primary weapon in this new era is the **False Claims Act (FCA)**, a powerful statute now being aggressively applied to combat customs fraud and tariff evasion. For importers, this means that simple errors in customs filings can now spiral into investigations carrying the threat of **treble damages** and staggering financial penalties, making robust compliance programs more critical than ever.

This crackdown is a direct response to sophisticated schemes designed to circumvent significant trade remedies, including **Section 301** tariffs and **antidumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD)**. The government's message is clear: the failure to pay customs duties is no longer just a regulatory issue for **U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP)**; it is considered fraud against the United States, and the **DOJ** will prosecute it accordingly.

## Key Developments

The primary development driving this trend is the strategic realignment of the **DOJ**'s Trade Fraud Task Force. Under new leadership, the task force has signaled a clear intent to prioritize customs cases, particularly those initiated by whistleblowers, known as qui tam relators, under the **FCA**. This has fundamentally changed the risk calculus for importers.

Unlike traditional **CBP** enforcement, which typically involves penalties equal to a multiple of the duties owed, an **FCA** action exposes a company to much more severe consequences. A defendant found liable under the **FCA** must pay three times the government's actual damages (the amount of unpaid duties) plus per-claim penalties, which in 2026 can exceed **$25,000** for each false entry document submitted. Given that a single shipment can involve multiple entries, these penalties can accumulate rapidly into millions of dollars.

The rise of whistleblower-initiated lawsuits is another key factor. Disgruntled employees, competitors, or supply chain partners with knowledge of non-compliance can file a qui tam lawsuit on behalf of the government and receive a substantial portion of any recovered funds. This creates a powerful financial incentive for insiders to report suspected tariff evasion, increasing the likelihood of an investigation.

## Enforcement Actions

The **DOJ**'s focus is on intentional or deliberately ignorant schemes to avoid paying customs duties. Recent enforcement actions have targeted a range of common non-compliance issues, including:

- **Systematic HTS Misclassification:** Deliberately classifying goods under an HTSUS subheading with a lower duty rate to evade tariffs.
- **Gross Undervaluation:** Falsely declaring a lower value for imported merchandise to reduce the ad valorem duty liability.
- **Country of Origin Fraud:** Using transshipment or other methods to conceal the true country of origin (e.g., China) to circumvent **Section 301** or **AD/CVD** orders.
- **False Free Trade Agreement Claims:** Claiming preferential duty treatment under an FTA without meeting the stringent rules of origin requirements.

Settlements in these cases frequently reach into the tens of millions of dollars. For example, a recent case saw an apparel importer pay over **$30 million** to resolve allegations of undervaluing goods to avoid duties, while another involving transshipped steel products resulted in a settlement exceeding **$50 million** after a whistleblower exposed the scheme.

## What Importers Must Do Now

In this heightened enforcement environment, a passive approach to compliance is no longer viable. Importers must take immediate, proactive steps to mitigate their risk of facing a devastating **FCA** lawsuit. A robust compliance strategy for 2026 should include:

1.  **Conduct Proactive Internal Audits:** Do not wait for a **CBP** notice or a **DOJ** subpoena. Implement a regular schedule of internal audits to review customs entries for accuracy in classification, valuation, and country of origin. Leveraging AI-powered auditing tools can help analyze vast amounts of data to identify patterns and anomalies that may indicate systemic risk.

2.  **Strengthen Your Compliance Program:** A paper-based compliance manual is insufficient. Your Import Compliance Program (ICP) must be a living system with clear procedures, senior management oversight, regular training for employees involved in the import process, and corrective action plans.

3.  **Vet Your Supply Chain:** The **FCA**'s 'knowing' standard includes reckless disregard for the truth. You are responsible for the information provided by your foreign suppliers and logistics partners. Conduct thorough due diligence on all parties in your supply chain and demand verifiable documentation for origin and value.

4.  **Prioritize Recordkeeping:** Maintain meticulous records for every import transaction for at least five years. This documentation is your first line of defense in an investigation and must be able to substantiate every declaration made on your customs entries.

5.  **Establish a Whistleblower Protocol:** Create a clear and confidential internal channel for employees to report compliance concerns without fear of retaliation. Addressing potential issues internally is far better than learning about them through a **DOJ** investigation initiated by a whistleblower.
