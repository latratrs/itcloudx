## The 2026 Sanctions & OFAC Landscape

As of March 2026, the global sanctions landscape is characterized by its heightened complexity, aggressive enforcement posture, and dynamic nature. Looking back at 2025 and into the first quarter of 2026, regulators, particularly OFAC, have continued to broaden their enforcement scope, focusing not just on sanctioned entities themselves but also on the facilitators, networks, and financial mechanisms that enable sanctions evasion. The trend observed in 2025, as highlighted by Foley Hoag, pointed towards an intensifying international trade enforcement environment, a trajectory that has only accelerated into 2026.

A central theme has been the relentless focus on regions and entities associated with destabilizing activities. Sanctions related to Russia's ongoing aggression in Ukraine remain a dominant feature, with new designations appearing consistently, as evidenced by Steptoe's "Weekly Sanctions Update" as recent as March 23, 2026. These designations target individuals, entities, and vessels involved in supporting the war effort, circumventing existing restrictions, or facilitating the transfer of prohibited technologies. Beyond Russia, OFAC, the EU, and the UN continue to vigorously enforce sanctions against regimes in Iran, Venezuela, and those involved in human rights abuses or dual-use technology proliferation, particularly concerning China and Hong Kong.

Enforcement actions in 2025 set the stage for 2026, underscoring the severity of violations. Corporate Compliance Insights reported on "The State of OFAC Sanctions Enforcement in 2025-26," noting a significant uptick in both the number and monetary value of penalties. One of the most telling examples from 2025 was a record-setting penalty, as detailed by JD Supra, which saw a major enforcement action by OFAC resulting in "2025’s Largest Penalty (So Far)." This multi-million dollar settlement served as a stark reminder that regulators are willing to pursue significant fines against entities found to be in violation, even if such violations are rooted in inadequate compliance controls rather than explicit intent. This particular case underscored the necessity for robust, automated screening mechanisms to prevent even indirect engagement with sanctioned parties.

Furthermore, 2025 saw a specific OFAC action against a freight forwarder, illustrating that no sector involved in international trade is immune (JD Supra). This case, alongside others noted by Crowell & Moring LLP in their "Month in International Trade – December 2025" review, signaled a clear message: the onus is on all participants in the supply chain to conduct thorough due diligence. The enforcement actions were not limited to traditional financial institutions but expanded to cover logistical services, virtual asset platforms, and technology providers. For instance, the DOJ and FinCEN reached resolutions with a virtual asset trading platform over AML violations (Corporate Compliance Insights), highlighting an expanding regulatory reach into emerging financial technologies that can be exploited for sanctions evasion. These developments in 2025 are predictive of continued and expanded scrutiny across all sectors in 2026, emphasizing the critical need for comprehensive compliance frameworks that account for both conventional and unconventional avenues of sanctions circumvention. The message is clear: regulators are enhancing their capabilities and casting a wider net to identify and penalize all actors who fail to uphold their sanctions compliance obligations.

## Real-World Impact on Importers and Freight Forwarders

For importers and freight forwarders, the escalating sanctions enforcement environment translates directly into tangible business impacts and significant operational risks. The dynamic nature of new designations, as seen in weekly updates from sources like Steptoe, means that a counterparty that was compliant last week could be designated this week, instantly jeopardizing ongoing shipments and relationships. This creates immense pressure to maintain real-time awareness and adapt rapidly.

A primary operational risk is the delay or outright seizure of shipments. If a freight forwarder inadvertently handles goods destined for or originating from a sanctioned entity, or if any party in the transaction (shipper, consignee, intermediate consignee, bank, vessel owner) becomes designated, the shipment can be detained at customs. This leads to costly delays, storage fees, potential loss of goods, and severe disruption to supply chains. The reputational damage from being associated with a sanctions violation can be immense, leading to loss of client trust, difficulty securing future contracts, and even blacklisting by financial institutions.

The case example of OFAC's action against a freight forwarder in 2025 (JD Supra) serves as a stark warning. This incident demonstrated that ignorance is not a defense; freight forwarders are expected to exercise due diligence to ensure they are not facilitating transactions with sanctioned parties, even if the primary contract is with an un-sanctioned entity. The financial exposure for non-compliance extends far beyond direct penalties. It encompasses legal fees for investigations and defense, the costs associated with unwinding or rerouting shipments, potential loss of goods, increased insurance premiums, and the intangible but significant cost of reputational harm. Companies failing to identify beneficial ownership or complex corporate structures used by sanctioned entities risk unwittingly becoming complicit in evasion schemes. The challenge is magnified by the proliferation of shell companies and sophisticated obfuscation tactics designed to mask true ownership, demanding advanced screening capabilities that go beyond simple name matching.

## Why Customs Brokers and Compliance Teams Are on Alert

The intensifying sanctions landscape places customs brokers and internal compliance teams at the forefront of defense against violations. There's a palpable shift in liability, with regulators increasingly expecting all parties in the supply chain to exercise robust due diligence, effectively pushing the burden of compliance downstream. This means customs brokers are no longer just facilitating trade; they are also critical gatekeepers responsible for ensuring the legality of transactions from a sanctions perspective. As highlighted in discussions surrounding "2025 in Review" (Foley Hoag), the expectation for comprehensive due diligence has broadened considerably, holding all intermediaries to a higher standard.

Documentation requirements have become more stringent than ever. Every transaction, every counterparty, and every step of the supply chain must be meticulously documented to demonstrate that adequate screening and due diligence were performed. In the event of an audit or investigation, the ability to produce a clear, auditable trail of compliance checks is paramount. Failure to maintain such records can itself be a violation, even if no underlying sanctions breach occurred. Compliance teams are under pressure to not only implement robust screening protocols but also to ensure flawless record-keeping to satisfy regulatory demands.

The risk of audits, especially from OFAC, has significantly increased. Regulators are actively pursuing investigations and are prepared to levy substantial penalties for non-compliance. These audits can be incredibly resource-intensive, diverting valuable time and personnel away from core business operations. Beyond financial penalties, severe cases of non-compliance can lead to criminal charges for individuals and corporate executives, emphasizing the gravity of the situation. Customs brokers, who touch countless transactions daily, face particular exposure if their compliance programs are not watertight. They must not only screen their direct clients but also understand the ultimate consignees, end-users, and financial flows associated with the goods they handle. The complexity arises from the constant evolution of sanctions lists and the sophisticated methods employed by bad actors to evade detection, necessitating continuous vigilance and advanced tools for detection.

## How to Stay Compliant: 5 Steps for 2026

Navigating the dynamic sanctions environment of 2026 requires a proactive and technologically informed approach. Here are five crucial steps for importers, freight forwarders, and compliance teams:

1.  **Enhance Your Screening Protocols:** Move beyond basic name-matching. Implement comprehensive screening that incorporates beneficial ownership, network analysis, and identifies potential linkages to sanctioned entities and their affiliates. This means looking beyond direct customers and suppliers to understand the entire ecosystem of a transaction.
2.  **Regularly Update Your Sanctions Knowledge:** Subscribe to official alerts from OFAC, the EU, and the UN. Continuously monitor updates to the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) List, Sectoral Sanctions Identifications (SSI) List, and other relevant sanctions regimes. This ensures your data is always current against the weekly updates observed in March 2026.
3.  **Implement Robust Internal Controls & Training:** Develop and enforce clear, written sanctions compliance policies and procedures within your organization. Conduct regular, mandatory training for all relevant employees—including sales, logistics, finance, and leadership—to ensure they understand their responsibilities and the severe consequences of non-compliance.
4.  **Conduct Due Diligence Beyond Direct Parties:** Extend your due diligence to all parties involved in a transaction, including ultimate consignees, end-users, vessel owners, and financial intermediaries. Utilize public records, corporate registries, and open-source intelligence to uncover potential red flags and verify the legitimacy of all entities in the supply chain.
5.  **Leverage Technology for Automation & Accuracy:** Manual screening is no longer sufficient.
